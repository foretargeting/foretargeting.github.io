TL;DR When learning from human videos, robots should not imitate human actions; they should imitate human intentions. Human videos lack the implicit forces needed to complete a task, but they show clearly what the human intended to do. We introduce Foretargeting, which splits imitation learning from human videos into two parts: learning what the human intended to do, and finding the forces needed to achieve that intention. For the first part, we learn an object-centric 3D intention simulator that predicts an object's future 6DoF poses from human hand actions. For the second part, at deployment, we roll out the intention simulator on the actions predicted by a behavior cloning (BC) policy trained purely on human data, and retarget the forecast hand–object trajectory to the robot with sampling-based model predictive control (MPC). This step both transfers the motion to the robot embodiment and recovers the forces needed to move the object as intended. Foretargeting enables dexterous tasks at which naive BC deployment fails, such as writing with a marker and sweeping with a brush.

Contributions

1Force-aware deployment of human-data policies

We address a central problem in learning from humans: applying the necessary forces without observing them.

2An intention simulator

To our knowledge, ours is the first world model to forecast 6DoF object trajectories given human hand actions, trained on real egocentric data.

3Fast, deterministic functional retargeting

Our retargeter keeps the robot hand human-like by aligning its bones with the human's, and carries the human's contacts onto it with optimal transport. It produces identical results across runs and is 3.9× faster than prior sampling-based methods, on a CPU.

Motivation

Copying human behavior does not reproduce its outcome. Human and robot hands differ in shape, and observed hand motion does not include the forces that make an interaction succeed.

Teleoperated robot data records commanded actions separately from the measured state, so the actions carry the force; a human video records only the state. We therefore separate intention from control: we learn from human play how objects should move, and leave to robot control how to reach that intention.

Method overview

A BC policy trained on human demonstrations proposes hand motions, our intention simulator predicts their consequences, and rolling out the two together gives a hand–object plan. Sampling-based MPC then searches for the robot commands that make the object follow it under the robot's own embodiment and contact dynamics, so the necessary forces emerge at deployment time.

Intention simulator

Given an object and a proposed hand trajectory, our intention simulator forecasts the object motion that the interaction is intended to produce.

A physics simulator cannot play this role: replaying observed hand trajectories under physics does not reproduce the demonstrated outcome. Our simulator instead learns how objects respond to hands from human experience, while staying physically grounded enough to avoid infeasible trajectories.

What goes in

Our model takes three inputs: (1) the object's shape as 512 points sampled from its mesh and encoded into a single token by a PointNet; (2) the three most recent object poses; and (3) the 21 3D landmarks of each hand over three past and three future frames, where the future frames are the hand actions. Frames are 0.1 s apart.

How hands meet the object

To each hand token we add interaction features computed from the observed object state: the position and velocity of every landmark relative to the object, and the vector and distance from the wrist and each fingertip to the nearest object surface.

How it predicts

We train an 11.5M-parameter transformer with flow matching: given noisy future object poses, our model predicts the velocity between the clean and the noisy poses, and at inference we integrate the learned ODE from noise with 4 Euler steps. It outputs the next three object poses, relative to the last observed pose. At test time, we roll it out autoregressively, feeding its predictions back in as the pose history; it forecasts 16× faster than real time. We train with the flow-matching loss and a geometric loss on object surface points.

What it learns from

We train on human–object play data, ObjectWorld. In the real world, collectors wear Aria Gen 2 glasses and interact with each of 423 objects for 5 minutes, cycling through a fixed set of interaction modes; we reconstruct each object's mesh, track its pose and discard poorly tracked segments. In simulation, collectors teleoperate bimanual MANO hands and move each of 30 objects toward random goal poses, and we keep the failures, which keep the model physically grounded. We rotate and shift each window on the desk, mirror it with the hands swapped, and add temporally correlated noise to the observed poses. We hold out 13 objects entirely.

Retargeting

We split retargeting into two stages, both on the CPU: a kinematic stage builds a human-like robot reference, with optimal transport fitting the human's contacts to the robot's own morphology, and a physical stage searches for the forces that move the object as forecast.

The kinematic stage solves for all frames of a long-horizon trajectory at once, in time linear in its length: bone directions give the robot the human's hand shape, unbalanced optimal transport carries the human's contacts, palm included, onto the robot's links, and penetration and smoothness terms clean the trajectory. The physical stage runs a receding-horizon cross-entropy search in MuJoCo for bounded corrections to this reference. Three rules keep the search cheap: fingers far from the object barely explore, corrections are smooth splines, and the search rewinds when the object drifts more than 6 cm from the forecast. Our retargeter therefore needs no GPU, is fully reproducible, and needs no reward shaping or per-task tuning.

A recorded or forecast hand is noisy: its fingers sink into the object or hover above it, and the tracking jitters. We clean it before physics.

We align every robot bone with the matching human bone, so the robot hand takes the human's shape whatever its finger lengths.

Why bone directions

We found that the hooked "spider hand" comes from fingertip-only IK on fingers of a different length than the human's, so we match bone directions instead; unlike matching keypoint vectors, no vector has to be rescaled, and the distal error on a flat hand drops from 41° to 5°. The closest related method, TopoRetarget (Wu et al., CoRL 2026), also uses bone directions, but only as a per-frame warm-up before an interaction-mesh QP, with hard non-penetration and no physics.

We use unbalanced optimal transport to match the human's contact points, palm included, to points on the robot hand, so the robot grasps where the human did all around the hand.

The full recipe

01
Reached reference

The BC-commanded MANO hand is played in the MANO simulator; the hand it reaches, not the raw command, is the reference.

02
Bone directions

Each robot bone points where the human bone points, so the hand keeps the human's shape whatever its finger lengths.

03
Contact fade

Fingertip targets fade in near the object, exp(−d / 12 mm). No thresholds, no contact labels.

04
Distances, not contacts

A softplus on pairwise signed distances keeps fingers out of the object and apart; touching fingers rest on it.

05
Whole motion at once

All frames in one least-squares problem, smoothness as a cost. Block-banded Gauss–Newton, linear in length.

06
Contact transport

Unbalanced optimal transport carries the human's contact, palm and phalanges included, onto the robot's links.

07
Feedforward

Inverse dynamics without contact, clipped to the actuator limits, so servos make the planned motion.

08
Static friction

An elliptic friction cone and 2 noslip iterations, so the object no longer creeps out of a still hand. (impratio 10 comes with the first Wuji scene.)

09
Calm CEM + recovery

1 s horizon, 0.15 s commit, 64 × 6. Exploration shrinks with distance to the object (3 % in the air), corrections are C² splines with 1/f² noise, so the hand stays human-like. If the object slips 6 cm, restore an earlier state and search harder.

We train one BC policy per task on human demonstrations and compare deploying it directly with deploying it through Foretargeting, on tasks such as writing with a marker and sweeping with a brush.

In the real world, we use two Franka arms facing each other with Wuji Hand 2.2 hands; in simulation, floating hands. We collect all task-specific data strictly with the human embodiment and retarget it at deployment time; we use no robot during training.

Producing BC Data

We produced 12 dexterous tasks in simulation and 6 on the real robot, with human demonstrations only. For each, we train an object-centric BC policy on point clouds of the object's surface, so it can act on a forecast pose as well as a tracked one.

In simulation, we build 12 dexterous tasks for manosim with agentic AI tools, and inject noise while generating their demonstrations so that they include recoveries.

Detailswhy Markov, the task videos, recovery takes, the numbers

Markov, so recovery comes free

The expert reads only the current state. After a push, a missed grasp or a dropped object, its next command is still the right one from wherever things are now. We put such an event into about 30 % of takes on purpose; we mark the ticks under it invalid, and the BC learns the recovery after it.

What each BC learns from

DART noise moves the hands off course and the expert corrects them. Next to each command we save the hand pose it reached. Our BC trains on these reached hand states, like any policy trained on human data; the commands themselves train only the Action BC oracle we compare against.

Checked before it is kept

We keep a take only if the task succeeds. Every kept demo replays bit-exact.

In the real world, we record a single human demonstration per task in the calibrated robot workspace, refine its hand and object tracking with agentic AI tools, and expand it to 2,000 demonstrations with MimicGen, covering a 50×60 cm workspace. This takes about an hour per task.

Discussion

We presented Foretargeting, which deploys policies trained on human data by forecasting the intended object motion and retargeting robot actions to follow it. Separating intent from control lets us improve the policy and the intention simulator independently of robot control. More human play data improves the intention simulator, giving the retargeter more accurate targets without force labels or robot data, and since the policy predicts human hand actions, the same approach can deploy stronger policies trained on larger human datasets.

Limitations

  • Open loop. We execute plans open loop, so we cannot correct our actions when the real object deviates from the forecast. Updating the forecast and retargeting from new observations is a natural next step.
  • One rigid object. Our intention simulator currently assumes a single rigid object with a known mesh on a tabletop.
  • Tracked, not motion capture. We train and evaluate our intention simulator using tracked object poses rather than motion-capture measurements.
